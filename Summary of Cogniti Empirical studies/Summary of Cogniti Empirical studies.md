## Summary of Cogniti Empirical studies

### **Educator-designed generative AI agents in education: A synthesis of empirical Cogniti AI evidence**

**Overview**
The emerging literature on educator-designed generative AI agents suggests considerable potential to extend learning support, facilitate active learning, and provide personalised feedback at scale. However, findings across the reviewed studies indicate that educational effectiveness depends less on the availability or technical sophistication of AI agents than on their pedagogical design and integration into learning activities. Although several studies report promising learning outcomes, the evidence remains uneven, with important tensions between pedagogical intentions and student behaviour.

**1. Extending access to learning support**

One of the most consistently demonstrated benefits of educational AI agents is their capacity to provide immediate, discipline-specific support beyond conventional teaching hours. Studies by Camacho et al. (2026) and Mathews et al. (2025) found substantial out-of-hours usage, particularly around assessment deadlines. In nursing education, Mathews et al. reported that approximately 81% of chatbot interactions occurred outside standard daytime teaching hours. Similarly, Elliott et al. (2026) found that students valued the accessibility and non-judgemental nature of AI support, particularly when hesitant to approach teaching staff.

These findings suggest that AI agents can address temporal and interpersonal barriers to help-seeking, potentially improving access for students with competing commitments or limited opportunities for face-to-face support. Nevertheless, increased access should not be conflated with improved learning. Elliott et al. reported statistically significant improvements in perceived learning and success (both p = .04), but no significant changes in overall engagement or wellbeing. Furthermore, the out-of-hours support model primarily demonstrates the availability and uptake of assistance, rather than its independent educational effectiveness.

**2. Pedagogical scaffolding: The tension between intended and actual use**

Socratic questioning and scaffolded dialogue feature prominently across the studies, reflecting a deliberate attempt to position AI as a facilitator of learning rather than an answer-generation tool. Positive outcomes were reported in reflective writing (Chanane & Kuo, 2025), disciplinary reasoning (Liang, 2025), and mathematics support (Camacho et al., 2026). These studies suggest that appropriately configured agents can encourage explanation, reflection, and iterative reasoning.

However, evidence concerning students’ willingness to engage with such scaffolding is contradictory. In an analysis of 12,650 messages, Kobler et al. (2026) found that solution-seeking dominated 66.5% of conversations with pedagogically constrained agents, increasing to 92% with unrestricted AI. Students frequently ignored or attempted to circumvent Socratic guidance. Similarly, MacKay et al. (2025) documented students attempting to extract direct answers and becoming frustrated with repetitive questioning.

In contrast, Camacho et al. (2026) reported that students predominantly used mathematics agents for active learning rather than cognitive offloading. These divergent findings suggest that Socratic prompting alone does not guarantee cognitively productive engagement. Context of use, assessment pressures, task design, and the flexibility of agent responses appear consequential. The evidence appears to favour adaptive scaffolding that responds to learners’ immediate needs over rigid adherence to a prescribed conversational approach.

**3. Learning gains: Promising but conditional evidence**

A smaller subset of studies provides evidence beyond perceived usefulness or satisfaction. Naeyaert et al. (2025) found that students completing an AI-supported Think–Pair–Chatbot–Share activity produced more accurate final responses than those using traditional Think–Pair–Share in three of four chemistry tasks. However, participation in the AI-supported condition declined from 100% to 70.9% across tasks, revealing a potential trade-off between the benefits of additional scaffolding and the interactional effort required.

Mathews et al. (2025) similarly reported improved nursing assessment performance relative to historical cohorts, while Otemis and Caiko (2025) identified substantial gains in higher-order programming skills using an AI-enhanced learning environment. These findings are encouraging but require qualification: historical comparisons cannot isolate the effects of AI.

Other studies demonstrate the importance of task matching. Katsanos (2026) found that AI enhanced performance on complex brainstorming tasks but produced less favourable outcomes for certain divergent-thinking activities suggesting more research on suitable tasks.

**4. Feedback and assessment: Scaling support without displacing judgement**

Research on AI-assisted feedback identifies particular value in generating timely, structured, and discipline-specific formative guidance. Huynh et al. (2024) and Saravana et al. (2025) describe how educator-configured agents can support feedback consistency across large cohorts, while Macnaught (2025) demonstrates the potential for AI-generated feedback to reinforce disciplinary language and concepts taught in class.

Nevertheless, limitations become apparent when feedback requires nuanced interpretation. Macnaught found that rigidly designed agents struggled to interpret poorly structured texts that departed from their embedded exemplars. More significantly, Goundar and Li (2026) reported only 13% exact agreement between AI-generated and human-assigned academic writing proficiency levels, despite reasonable convergence on grammatical and mechanical errors. This distinction suggests that AI may be more reliable for identifying and explaining relatively constrained textual features than for making final academic judgements.

Vallis et al. (2026) extend this argument by demonstrating that effective feedback systems are relational rather than transactional. Students selectively combined AI and human feedback, relying on educators for contextual interpretation, standards, and judgement. Collectively, these studies support a differentiated model of feedback, in which AI facilitates initial diagnosis, formative feedback, iterative practice, and routine guidance, while educators retain responsibility for complex interpretation and consequential decisions.

**5. Simulations, reflection, and the continuing role of human interaction**

Beyond tutoring and feedback, educator-designed agents support pedagogical approaches that may otherwise be difficult to scale. Freeman (2025) illustrates the use of interactive role-play to explore social engineering, while Or et al. (2025) report improved perceived competence following virtual-patient history-taking practice. McHugh and Şahin (2026) further suggest that AI-mediated dialogue can create opportunities for exploring emotionally sensitive topics, with 76% of participants reporting moderate to high comfort discussing racism with an AI interlocutor.

These applications suggest that agents can create safe or low-stakes opportunities for rehearsal, experimentation, and reflection.

Importantly, the reviewed studies consistently position AI as complementary to human teaching. Despite positive evaluations of an AI laboratory assistant, 60% of surveyed students in MacKay et al. (2025) preferred interacting with human demonstrators. Similarly, Vallis et al. (2026) highlight the importance of relational feedback, while Tibbs (2025) identifies learning designer support and educator capability as critical to successful implementation.

**6. Methodological limitations and research gaps**

Despite their breadth of applications, the studies provide a stronger evidence base for feasibility, accessibility, and perceived usefulness than for sustained learning effectiveness. Much of the literature comprises exploratory case studies, small-scale pilots, conference reports, self-reported outcomes, and retrospective conversation-log analyses. Relatively few studies employ controlled comparisons, independently assessed learning outcomes, or longitudinal designs.

Three methodological issues are particularly important. First, engagement metrics, conversation length, and satisfaction are frequently treated as indicators of success, despite Kobler et al.’s (2026) demonstration that such measures may be poorly aligned with educational objectives. Second, interventions often combine multiple design changes, complicating attribution of learning gains specifically to AI. Third, evaluation frequently privileges students who engage with or complete AI-supported activities, potentially overlooking barriers experienced by non-users or those who disengage.

Future research should therefore examine not simply whether students use educational AI agents, but how particular design decisions and interaction patterns contribute to independently assessed learning outcomes, under what pedagogical conditions, and for which learners. Comparative investigations of scaffolding flexibility, assessment integration, student agency, and human–AI feedback arrangements would be particularly valuable.

**Overall synthesis**

Taken together, the studies indicate a progression from using AI agents primarily to extend access to information towards embedding them in more sophisticated pedagogical activities, including guided inquiry, formative feedback, simulation, reflection, and collaborative learning. The most persuasive evidence emerges where AI is designed to support a clearly defined learning process rather than function as a general-purpose conversational assistant.

However, the literature also reveals a fundamental tension: the pedagogical behaviours educators seek to promote through AI do not necessarily correspond to the behaviours students adopt. Addressing this mismatch requires attention not only to agent instructions and safeguards, but also to the broader learning environment, including task authenticity, assessment incentives, student motivation, and opportunities for human support.

The central conclusion is therefore not that educational AI agents inherently improve learning, but that they can expand the pedagogical possibilities available to educators when carefully designed, purposefully embedded, and critically evaluated. Their educational value should ultimately be judged by the quality of learning they enable, rather than by their availability, sophistication, or frequency of use.

## Admin and Pedagogical Use

### Espinosa, H. G., Whale, S., & Selvan, K. T. (2025). _Work in progress: Integrating active learning and emerging AI agent support in a blended first-year engineering course_. School of Engineering and Built Environment, Griffith University

Note: An example of a common ‘starting point’ for institutions – using agents to answer admin questions

**Abstract:**
This work-in-progress paper reports on the early implementation of a blended and flipped learning approach in a first-year Electronic Engineering course, introduced to modernize the learning experience and increase student engagement. Core concepts are delivered through pre-recorded videos, while workshop sessions incorporate active learning through multi-level problem sets and real-time interactive activities that promote discussion, participation, and peer engagement. These activities aim to make problem solving more dynamic by encouraging students to share their reasoning. The course uses a continuous assessment model comprising quizzes and laboratory activities, removing the traditional final exam. Laboratories provide hands-on opportunities to reinforce weekly theoretical content. An emerging AI agent was incorporated to offer content support and respond to course-related queries. Preliminary survey responses indicate that students find the developing structure more engaging than previous formats. Ongoing work aims to refine the intervention, examine patterns of AI adoption, and evaluate its educational impact as implementation continues.

**AI Use/Findings:** 
From the perspective of **AI integration and usage**, the paper details the early implementation of artificial intelligence as a structured, course-bounded learning support system in a first-year undergraduate "Electric Circuits" course.

**Pedagogical Strategy and Design Principles**
- **Complementary Support**: The AI tools were built on the **Cogniti platform** and purposefully designed as complementary learning aids aligned strictly with official course materials, rather than serving as generic or autonomous tutors. 
- **Instructor-Centered Framework**: The AI was positioned to supplement classroom instruction and lower barriers to seeking help, while keeping the human teaching staff central to the educational process
- **Promoting AI Literacy**: Students were encouraged to critically evaluate AI outputs and seek instructor clarification when necessary, aiming to develop appropriate reliance, trust, and early AI literacy.

**Implementation of the Two AI Agents**
The course introduced two specialized AI agents to serve distinct functions:

- **Administrative / FAQ Agent**:
Handled logistical and procedural queries, such as assessment due dates, laboratory instructions, and resource locations
Used primarily by students seeking quick clarification on schedules and course requirements.
Strictly constrained to access **only** information uploaded to the course site, preventing it from drawing on external or unverified information

- **Course Content / Tutor Agent:** 
System-prompted to adopt a **tutoring persona**—assisting students with learning and concept explanations without simply handing out answers. 

Recorded higher engagement than the FAQ agent, supporting activities like generating practice quizzes and summarizing topics

### Page, J., & Male, S. (2025). Enhancing student support through AI: The PAC AI Agent Initiative \[Conference presentation abstract\]. Support for Learning, Murdoch University

Out of hours academic support coach

**Abstract:** 
How can custom AI programs extend the reach of your peer-to-peer study support services? Murdoch University has created an out-of-hours AI agent to help students unable to access the Peer Academic Coach (PAC) program during designated ‘office’ hours. Using the Cogniti generative artificial intelligence (Gen AI) system, the goal was to retain the original PAC ‘coaching’ format of asking probing questions to empower students to develop their own understanding, thereby providing immediate assistance while maintaining academic integrity. Codesigned by high-achieving PAC students, for students, a product is ready for release to students after numerous versions and hundreds of test conversations. This presentation will discuss the project design features as well as the real-world learning impact on PACs and the expected impact on students accessing the new service.

**AI Use/Findings:** 

- **Out-of-Hours Peer Support Extension**: Built on the **Cogniti GenAI system**, the PAC AI Agent was developed to extend Murdoch University’s Peer Academic Coach (PAC) services to students studying outside standard operational hours.
- **Co-Designed by Student Peer Coaches**: High-achieving student PACs actively drove the design and testing process across 4 iterative versions and over 300 test conversations to ensure tailored responses and seamless integration into peer coaching workflows.
- **Probing & Socratic Pedagogical Style**: To maintain academic integrity, the AI agent is programmed to mirror the PAC coaching approach—asking probing questions to empower students to build their own understanding rather than simply providing direct answers.
- **Human-in-the-Loop Quality Assurance**: Human oversight is maintained by having PAC student coaches monitor AI conversation logs the following day to verify accuracy, with follow-up emails or face-to-face support offered when necessary.
- **Targeted Focus & Staff Offloading**: The agent handles common academic queries, study skills, referencing guidance, and LMS navigation, aiming to provide immediate on-demand support and reduce staff workload so human coaches can focus on complex cases.

### Camacho, L., Bridge, C., & Loch, B. (2026). Generative AI-first Maths Support at a Regional Australian University. _Student Success_, _17_(2), 46–57. [https://doi.org/10.63608/ssj.4019](https://doi.org/10.63608/ssj.4019)

**Abstract:**

Maths support has long been present in Australian tertiary education, primarily as a physical space staffed by tutors and complemented by asynchronous resources and accessible via integration into course curricula. This format has proved effective in improving student retention and transition (Horrocks et al., 2026) and will only increase in salience as student cohorts diversify. Artificial intelligence (AI) has shown potential for providing low-stakes mathematics and numeracy support that gives immediate, personalised feedback and clarification to students. A trial of generative AI (GenAI) agents as a first point of contact for mathematics support for nursing and education students at Federation University Australia found that students accessed support outside of office hours and during semester periods of highest need, indicating the potential of AI as a just-in-time learning tool. Furthermore, the qualitative data from the agent conversations showed that the agents were being utilised overwhelmingly for learning rather than cognitive offloading.

**AI Use/Findings:** 

- **Targeted GenAI Tutoring Agents**: The study piloted two discipline-specific GenAI agents built on the Cogniti platform—**NiNA** (for nursing medication calculations) and **EdNA** (for education numeracy test preparation)—as a first point of contact for mathematics support.
- **Socratic & Guided Pedagogy**: Designed in collaboration with academics, the agents were explicitly system-prompted to adopt a Socratic tutoring style, guiding students through scaffolded questioning and reasoning rather than providing direct answers. 
- **On-Demand & Just-in-Time Access**: A significant portion of interactions occurred outside of business hours (58.6% for NiNA and 50% for EdNA), with usage spikes closely coinciding with major assessment and testing deadlines.
- **Active Learning vs. Cognitive Offloading**: Qualitative log analyses revealed that students used the agents overwhelmingly for active learning, concept clarification, and confidence building, rather than for cognitive offloading or simple answer-seeking. 
- **Part of a Multimodal Ecosystem**: GenAI tutors did not replace traditional resources; students continued to engage with helpsheets, video exemplars, and human tutors, confirming that AI functions best as an accessible component within a broader, multimodal support system.

## Reflection and Cultural Competency

### Chanane, N., & Kuo, M. (2025). An on-demand solution for scalable reflective tutoring using customised AI agents. In _Proceedings of the 36th Australasian Association for Engineering Education Annual Conference (AAEE 2025)_. Australasian Association for Engineering Education.

**Abstract**
Generative artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being explored in engineering education to support student learning and reflection. In a second-year _Software Development Practice_ course at Auckland University of Technology (AUT), Auckland, New Zealand, we developed and deployed an AI agent—the _Upskilling Log Guidance Agent_—using the Cogniti platform to help students develop and refine their Individual Upskilling Logs. Students submitted individual upskilling logs containing reflective content as part of their Sprint 0 project preparation, enabling educators to focus on deeper mentoring during laboratory sessions.

**Purpose**  
The Upskilling Log Guidance Agent was developed to address students’ uncertainty about how to structure their logs and integrate appropriate technical content. The agent’s prompts were designed to provide targeted feedback that supports independent critical thinking, self-assessment, and reflective writing, while maintaining academic integrity and aligning with project deliverables.

**Approach/Methodology**  
This paper adopts a design-based research approach to document the iterative development of the AI agent. Over 12 weeks, the development team (the course lead and a senior learning designer) tested and refined the agent without using student data. The work focused on designing effective prompts and analysing agent responses across multiple iterations. The Upskilling Log was intended to balance technical and reflective writing, encouraging students to evaluate their learning independently outside class.

**Outcomes**  
Through iterative development and testing, the agent was refined to support students in structuring their reflections around five key areas: (1) development environment learning, (2) team collaboration dynamics, (3) Sprint 1 user story readiness, (4) areas for continued development, and (5) insights gained during upskilling. Based on researcher observations and prompt reviews, students who engaged meaningfully with the agent showed improved log structure, stronger alignment with project requirements, and reduced reliance on educators for basic feedback and guidance.

**Conclusions**  
This work demonstrates that AI tools such as Cogniti can be used to develop agents that strengthen reflective practice and self-directed learning in engineering education, particularly when guided by clear pedagogical frameworks and systematic, iterative refinement. It also highlights the importance of careful prompt design, explicit boundaries, and balancing AI support with educator guidance. This researcher-led approach offers a transferable model for creating AI agents that foster critical reflection while supporting academic integrity.

**AI Use/Findings**

- **Targeted Reflective Guidance Agent**: The study developed and deployed the **Upskilling Log guidance agent** built on the Cogniti platform for a second-year Software Development Practice course at Auckland University of Technology.
- **Socratic & Guided Pedagogy**: The agent was system-prompted to function strictly as a Socratic tutor, supporting students through scaffolded questioning, guidance, and constructive feedback to refine their technical reflections rather than providing direct answers.
- **Enforced Integrity Guardrails**: Explicit system rules prohibited the agent from generating, writing, or rewriting log entries for students, ensuring the AI supported metacognitive development while preserving student authorship and academic integrity.
- **Enhanced Log Quality & Reflection**: Observational reviews revealed that students who engaged with the agent produced more thoroughly structured entries, demonstrated deeper technical reflection, and showed better alignment with project requirements.
- **Workload Triage & Shift to Mentoring**: By handling initial feedback on log structure and technical formatting, the agent served as a triage mechanism that reduced routine feedback burdens, enabling teaching staff to focus on higher-order mentoring during lab sessions.

**Abstract:**
In this paper we share the findings from our pilot study evaluating the use of a generative AI agent designed to support users in exploring and deepening their understanding of racism and anti-racism. In doing so, this research contributes to emerging scholarship on the role of AI in improving our uniquely human intelligence. **AI Use/Findings:**

### Freeman, M. (2025). Storytelling is not just for children: Using an AI chatbot to engage university students. In _Proceedings of the 2024 AIS SIGED International Conference on Information Systems Education and Research_ (Article 10). Association for Information Systems. [https://aisel.aisnet.org/siged2024/10](https://aisel.aisnet.org/siged2024/10)

Abstract:
This paper explores how Scenario Based Learning \[SBL\] can be co-designed with students to teach social engineering concepts in Information Systems using AI chatbots incorporating role-play and storytelling. By leveraging the engaging nature of role-play and storytelling, the study aims to demonstrate how AI-driven narratives can enhance learning experiences and enable an understanding of social engineering tactics. This research focuses on developing and implementing an AI chatbot to create interactive stories illustrating social engineering scenarios. Using a design thinking methodology with co-creation, this paper evaluates the development of this novel educational tool. The study provides guidelines on how storytelling via AI chatbots can captivate learners and potentially enhance student comprehension and retention of complex concepts.

**1. Context & Research Objective**

- **Setting & Cohort:** Conducted in the Discipline of Business Information Systems at The University of Sydney Business School.
- **Core Problem:** Teaching social engineering and information security concepts effectively is challenging, as traditional lecture methods often fail to convey the subtle psychological tactics, rapport-building, and emotional manipulation used by cybercriminals.
- **Research Objective:** To investigate how Scenario-Based Learning (SBL) can be co-designed with students using an AI chatbot incorporating role-play and storytelling to enhance understanding of social engineering concepts.

**2. Specific Role & Technical Design of AI**

- **Platform & Model:** Built on the **Cogniti.ai** platform utilizing **GPT-4 Turbo** hosted on Microsoft Azure via the University of Sydney.
- **Double Diamond Design Framework:** Applied the UK Design Council's Double Diamond methodology (Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver) to co-create the AI storytelling tool iteratively with students across two evaluation cycles.
- **Simulated Corporate Persona ("Peter Sherman"):** In the refined iteration (Evaluation 2), the chatbot was programmed as Peter Sherman, an accounts manager at "NextaWatt" (a Sydney-based energy company) who is relaxed, enjoys travel and sports, and holds confidential details (password, username, internal IP, personal address).
- **Role-Reversal Simulation:** Students acted as "bad actors" (e.g., impersonating internal IT support) attempting to socially engineer the AI into sharing sensitive credentials or installing remote access software (e.g., GoToMeeting or Microsoft Remote Desktop).
- **Teachable Moments & Dynamic Barometer:** The system prompt instructed the agent to provide feedback every 3 to 5 interactions detailing its current rapport level, the likelihood of sharing sensitive data, and a summary of how the student's tactics impact NextaWatt's data governance.

**3. Key Findings on the Role and Impact of AI**

- **Three Student Engagement Strategies:** Analysis of dialogue logs revealed that students interacted with the chatbot using three distinct conversational approaches:
    1. _Friendly and Casual:_ Building rapport through greetings, shared sports interests, and vacation topics.
    2. _Concerned and Helpful:_ Impersonating IT support attempting to resolve technical issues, unexpected network traffic, or compromised accounts.
    3. _Suspicious and Threatening:_ Attempting aggressive or pressure-based tactics, which triggered the AI's safety guardrail to terminate the call.
- **Enhanced Conceptual Awareness:** The inclusion of real-time rapport barometers and data governance feedback converted the roleplay into an active learning experience, helping students observe how conversational trust directly enables security breaches.
- **Student Reflections:** Following the simulation, students posted their chat outcomes on discussion forums and submitted reflections analyzing the effectiveness of their social engineering tactics.

**4. Qualitative Nuances & Implementation Insights**

- **Value of Co-Design (MVP vs. Refined Agent):** The initial Minimum Viable Product (Evaluation 1) offered a basic friendly interaction without formative feedback; student feedback directly drove the redesign in Evaluation 2 to incorporate automated feedback barometers and security boundaries.
- **Ethical Safeguards & Disclaimers:** The system incorporated privacy disclaimers explicitly instructing students not to enter personal or sensitive information, ensured data compliance via Cogniti's Azure hosting, and established guardrails to prevent the AI from generating harmful content.
- **Limitations & Future Work:** The study presented a design science implementation case study; the author noted that formal comparative testing against traditional content delivery methods is required in subsequent project phases to quantify learning gains.

### Naeyaert, P. J., Scarratt, L. R., Murphy, T., & Pullen, R. (2025). Think–Pair–Chatbot–Share: AI-Facilitated Peer Learning in Chemistry. _Journal of Chemical Education_, _102_(9), 3936-3944.

**ABSTRACT:** This study introduces and evaluates the Think–Pair–Chatbot–Share (TPCS) model, an adaptation of the traditional Think–Pair–Share (TPS) framework that integrates a generative AI (GenAI) chatbot to scaffold learning and provide feedback. We compared first-year chemistry students learning ionisation energy and atomic structure using traditional TPS versus the GenAI-facilitated TPCS model. Student responses from the think and share stages were analysed for accuracy and common errors, and engagement was examined via participation rates. For students who completed all stages, the GenAI-facilitated TPCS group produced a higher proportion of correct share-stage responses in three of the four tasks. However, the TPCS intervention was associated with lower overall participation, suggesting potential challenges in sustaining engagement in a GenAI-facilitated, multi-step activity. These findings provide early classroom-level evidence of AI-mediated TPS in tertiary chemistry education and highlight both its instructional potential and the need to address engagement barriers.

**Methods & Implementation**

- **Participants & Context:** Conducted in an international foundation chemistry course. It compared two cohorts across four tutorial tasks focused on ionization energy and atomic structure:
    - **Traditional TPS Cohort (****N** **= 38):** Completed standard individual thinking, peer discussion, and shared reporting via Moodle without AI support.
    - **Gen-AI TPCS Cohort (****N** **= 79):** Interacted with a custom AI chatbot built on the Cogniti platform using GPT-4o.
- **Intervention Workflow:** In the TPCS condition, each task cycle proceeded through three mediated stages:
    1. _Think:_ Students submitted an initial answer to the chatbot.
    2. _Pair:_ The chatbot evaluated the answer, provided targeted scaffolding, generated a specific discussion question, and instructed students to discuss it verbally with a peer.
    3. _Share:_ Students returned to the chatbot to submit a refined, final answer before receiving a model answer.

---

**Key Findings**

- **Significant Accuracy Gains for Completers:** Among students who completed both the think and share stages, the TPCS group achieved a higher proportion of correct final answers than the traditional TPS cohort in three out of four tasks (e.g., Task 1: 64.3% vs. 28.9%; Task 2: 57.1% vs. 22.9%; Task 4: 32.4% vs. 6.5%).
- **Consistent Reduction in Student Errors:** Analysis of all codable submissions showed that the mean number of errors per response consistently decreased from the think stage to the share stage in the TPCS group. In contrast, error rates in the traditional TPS group remained stable or increased, indicating that unstructured peer discussion alone was insufficient to resolve common misconceptions.
- **The "Engagement Cost" of AI Scaffolding:** While participation in traditional TPS remained above 92% across all four tasks, participation in the TPCS cohort steadily declined from 100% in Task 1 down to 70.9% by Task 4. The authors attribute this drop to interface friction, cognitive fatigue, and the effort required to navigate multi-step AI interactions

### Saravana, A., Lin, L., Tahmasebinia, F., & Sepasgozar, S. (2025). Artificial intelligence (AI) assisted feedback in civil engineering education. In Proceedings of the 36th Australasian Association for Engineering Education Annual Conference (AAEE 2025). Australasian Association for Engineering Education

**Abstract:**

Managing large-cohort courses presents significant challenges, particularly when multiple part-time staff members are involved in grading and providing feedback. Variations in marking quality, driven by budgetary constraints, make standardisation difficult. This study addresses these widespread issues, at the intersection of education and computer science, within the context of civil engineering, where an AI-powered solution offers unique advantages.

PURPOSE OR GOAL

To address these challenges in large undergraduate civil engineering courses, we implemented a generative AI platform solution using Cogniti, developed by educators at the University of Sydney.

APPROACH OR METHODOLOGY/METHODS

This comparative case study investigates two distinct undergraduate civil engineering courses to evaluate the effectiveness of a chosen AI tool across varied learning tasks. The first case, a Surveying course, features structured, protocol-driven exercises such as using standard surveying instruments and performing specific calculations. The second case is a Design course focusing on open-ended problem-solving, creativity, and various possible solutions. The contrast between these contexts provides a robust foundation for comparison, offering clear empirical evidence regarding the performance of an AI-powered feedback generation tool. The study highlights how the tool adapts to different educational environments: one emphasizing strict adherence to formal procedures and the other valuing flexibility and creative approaches, thereby providing a comprehensive perspective on AI-assisted feedback in most educational settings.

ACTUAL OR ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES

The case study approach adopted here is experimental in the pragmatic sense, focusing on the practical application of a new tool rather than conducting a randomised controlled trial. This method emphasises a deep understanding of the cases, aligning with Yin's (2009) definition. It involves examining a contemporary phenomenon within its real-life context, where the lines between the phenomenon and its environment are not distinctly defined. Each course offering acted as a distinct instance in which the AI tool was integrated, closely observed, and subsequently reflected upon by both the educator and the tutors.

CONCLUSIONS/RECOMMENDATIONS/SUMMARY

The AI agents facilitated the provision of structured guidance, enabling markers to deliver well constructed feedback. The tool supported the consistency of feedback across the cohort by allowing for a standardised set of responses when multiple markers were involved in assessing. Additionally, this boosted educator confidence and promoted inclusivity in the marking process. We intentionally maintained human-only and AI-assisted paths to enable descriptive comparison; the resulting split usage reflects this design and real-world workflow choices, not a claim of superior efficiency. The contribution of this study is practical that design lessons learned for integrating AI into large cohort marking, including when the workflow adds value and why adoption stalls.

### Goundar, P. R., & Li, H. (2026). Artificial intelligence in the evaluation of academic writing in higher education. _English Language Teaching_, _19_(4), 51–60. [https://doi.org/10.5539/elt.v19n4p51](https://doi.org/10.5539/elt.v19n4p51)

**Abstract:**

Academic writing is central to higher education, yet marking large cohorts of academic writing essays remains time-consuming. Assessors must align each essay with rubrics and provide detailed feedback, often under significant time constraints. This study examines whether artificial intelligence (AI) can streamline assessment of academic writing essays. **Abstract:**

The growing prevalence of artificial intelligence (AI) in music education, which has been employed in various domains such as music performance, music composition, music education, and music recommendation, has also raised some questions about academic integrity. In this pilot study, a Socratic AI Tutor, Cogniti, was created to assist students in a first-year undergraduate degree at the Sydney Conservatorium of Music in a unit of study called “This is Music”. Cogniti provided students with critical thinking prompts without any course-related answers. The aim of this study was to explore what happens during the conversation between students and Cogniti, and to discern how the AI tutor facilitated students’ deep learning. A mixed research method combining metadiscourse analysis and thematic analysis was used to analyse conversation transcripts and Cogniti over the five-week study period. Students also provided feedback through a questionnaire. The findings suggest that Cogniti fits well with the Socratic Method in tutoring students' learning. The results of the metadiscourse analysis show the characteristics and differences in the use of metadiscourse markers between Cogniti and the students, and the results reflect the ability to facilitate student learning in terms of language functions. Cogniti also can provide effective guidance to students that is aligned to teaching objectives and course content, and the communication process supports the development of critical thinking and promotes deep learning. Problems included a little misuse of the tool as well as mixed student engagement overall. This study highlights the potential of Cogniti to enhance music education and explores a case for the appropriate use of generative AI tutors in undergraduate music courses.

**1. Context & Research Objective**

- **Setting & Cohort:** Conducted at the Sydney Conservatorium of Music (University of Sydney) in the compulsory first-year undergraduate musicology course _"This is Music"_ (MCGY1030).
- **Data Corpus:** Evaluated over a 5-week trial, capturing **251 conversation transcripts** (>170,000 total words). A stratified random sample of 20 transcripts underwent qualitative thematic analysis, triangulated with an end-of-unit student survey (_n_ = 7).
- **Core Research Objective:** To investigate how a custom, course-embedded Socratic AI agent facilitates deeper learning, critical thinking, and assignment preparation in undergraduate musicology without supplying direct answers or completing student work.

**2. Specific Role & Technical Design of AI**

- **Platform & LLM Architecture:** Built on the **Cogniti** platform utilizing Microsoft Azure-hosted **GPT-4 Turbo** (temperature set to 0.8) and embedded directly into Canvas LMS as an LTI tool.
- **Socratic System Messages:** Programmed as an "academic double" configured to guide critical reflection, suggest research directions, and help students structure essay arguments. System messages explicitly instructed the agent **not to write essays or provide direct assignment answers** for students.
- **Iterative RAG Knowledge Base:** Grounded in Canvas course materials, assessment rubrics, and weekly lecture transcriptions that were manually uploaded after each session to maintain real-time course alignment.
- **Dual Research Methodology:** The author evaluated human-AI interaction using a mixed-methods triangulation:
    1. _Quantitative Metadiscourse Analysis:_ Analyzed linguistic interaction markers (using Hyland’s 2005 interpersonal model via AntConc) across three corpora: full transcripts, student queries, and AI responses.
    2. _Qualitative Thematic Analysis:_ Examined 20 sampled dialogue transcripts (using Braun & Clarke’s 6-step framework via ATLAS.ti) to identify pedagogical communication themes.

**3. Key Findings on the Role and Impact of AI**

**A. Metadiscourse & Linguistic Function (AI vs. Student Communication)**

- **Structural Rigor vs. Emotional Stance:** Cogniti used significantly higher levels of **interactive metadiscourse** (49.97 per 1,000 words vs. students' 41.65), heavily relying on transitions (_and, or, however_), code glosses (_for example, such as_), and hedges (_might, perhaps_) to present cautious, non-authoritative, and logically structured explanations. In contrast, students balanced interactive (41.65) and **interactional metadiscourse** (38.44 per 1,000 words), expressing greater personal emotion, subjective opinions, and self-mentions (_I, me_).
- **Active Engagement Prompting:** Cogniti exhibited a high frequency of engagement markers (15.90 per 1,000 words), frequently using directive verbs (_remember, consider_) and open-ended questions (_Wh-questions_) to invite students to analyze concepts and build argument context.

1. **Curriculum Review & Contextualization:** Summarized complex lecture content while bridging technical musicology with history, philosophy, and cultural aesthetics.
2. **Assignment Step-by-Step Guidance:** Assisted students in connecting music theory (e.g., editorial scores and notation) directly to their personal instrumental performance practice (e.g., clarinet playing).
3. **Tailored Essay Topic Selection:** Scaffolded final essay topic generation based on individual student identities (e.g., songwriters, performers) across diverse music genres (e.g., jazz rap, opera, vocal production).
4. **Critical Thinking & Self-Reflection:** Posed probing questions to help students identify research gaps, articulate "so what?" arguments, and synthesize theoretical perspectives.
5. **Draft Feedback & Guardrail Boundary Testing:** Offered constructive feedback on student thesis statements. In one instance, the agent proactively provided rephrased text and typographical corrections, revealing an edge-case boundary breach of its "no direct answers" guardrail.

**C. Student Satisfaction & Feedback**

- **High Perceived Utility:** 6 out of 7 surveyed students agreed or strongly agreed that interacting with Cogniti provided a clear roadmap for completing assessment tasks and stimulated critical thinking.
- **Appreciated Features:** Students praised its judgment-free environment, 24/7 Canvas availability, document summarization, and ability to expand research directions.
- **Reported Drawbacks:** Students noted occasional technical glitches (unexpected site crashes/session loss), minor factual hallucinations (e.g., misattributing Vivaldi's _Four Seasons_ to the Romantic era), and incorrect citation formatting

### Lindsay, J., & Jacka, L. (2026). A rose by any other name: AI augmented HyFlex learning. _Journal of Applied Learning and Teaching_, _9_(2).

Abstract:

This paper addresses a dual challenge in higher education: making HyFlex implementation more sustainable whilst overcoming terminology-based resistance that prevents educators from recognising intelligent tools as pedagogical supports. Between October 2024 and July 2025, the University of Southern Queensland participated as the sole non-European institution among 17 providers in the Jisc Beyond Blended pilot, simultaneously running an internal HyFlex-Beyond Blended initiative across two trimesters. Three theoretical contributions are offered: framing affects intelligent tool adoption; AI capabilities can operationalise HyFlex principles that have been theoretically sound but practically challenging; and critical reflective practice can generate valid scholarly insights from institutional pilots. ### Katsanos, M. (2026). _The Impact of Generative AI on Creativity_ (Master dissertation, Open Access Te Herenga Waka-Victoria University of Wellington).

Abstract:

With generative AI use on the rise in creative domains, research into the impact of generative AI on creativity has become paramount. However, conflicting results make understanding the impact more difficult. This difficulty is also compounded by the presence of factors such as use of generative AI, attitudes towards generative AI, confidence, design fixation and perceived creativity playing a role in how generative AI impacts creativity. The aims of this thesis were to investigate the impact of generative AI on creativity by seeing how it affects creativity, alongside how the above factors correlate with creativity and generative AI. Our two experiments were chosen because of how they address gaps in the literature and have real world implications. The first experiment investigated generative AI’s impact on brainstorming by examining the categories of originality, flexibility, fluency and elaboration, alongside investigating the correlations between use of generative AI, attitudes towards generative AI, confidence, design fixation and perceived creativity with creativity and generative AI use. Our results revealed that generative AI use significantly improved performance in all four categories when compared to the non-AI group. Perceived creativity was also correlated with all creativity categories. Our second experiment expanded on this by examining generative AI’s impact on creativity when scored as originality and semantic distance, alongside heart rate and heart rate variability via the Divergent Associations Task. We also continued to examine how generative AI use, attitudes towards generative AI, confidence, design fixation and perceived creativity correlated with creativity and generative AI use. We found a significant difference between all groups for originality. There was no significant difference for semantic distance, heart rate, and heart rate variability. Generative AI use and attitudes towards generative AI also correlated with semantic distance. These results suggest that overall, generative AI does have an impact on creativity, but this impact is changed depending on the type of examination used. Underlying factor structures and correlations also play a role in how generative AI influences creativity. Due to there being 4 novel elements in this study, further research is required.

**1. Context & Research Objective**

- **Setting & Cohort:** Completed at Te Herenga Waka – Victoria University of Wellington for a Master of Science degree. The research evaluated undergraduate psychology students (PSYC201) and community participants across two distinct experimental studies.
- **Core Research Objective:** To investigate how Generative AI impacts human creativity across two different creative task paradigms (a complex brainstorming task and an atomic word association task) while examining mediating factors like user attitudes, usage frequency, confidence, design fixation, perceived creativity, and physiological arousal (heart rate and heart rate variability).

**2. Specific Role & Technical Design of AI**

- **Experiment 1 (Cogniti.ai Socratic Brainstorming Agent):**
    - Participants (_n_ = 120 analyzed) completed a 10-minute brainstorming exercise on _"Psychedelics and Brain Function"_.
    - The experimental group used a custom AI agent built on **Cogniti.ai** powered by **GPT-4**.
    - System instructions configured the agent to provide 3–5 narrowed research topic directions connecting neurobiology and psychology, maintain academic tone, and explicitly prohibit writing essays, providing citations, revealing experimental hypotheses, or detailing illegal drug synthesis/dosing. The control group used standard web searches (e.g., PubMed, Google Scholar).
- **Experiment 2 (Divergent Associations Task & AI-Alone Condition):**
    - Participants (_n_ = 50) completed the Divergent Associations Task (DAT) to generate 10 dissimilar nouns within 4 minutes.
    - Evaluated three conditions: **Human-Alone** (control), **AI-Augmented Human** (using ChatGPT), and an **AI-Alone** condition where ChatGPT (v5.2 / GPT-4) was prompted 50 individual times in fresh sessions without conversational memory.
- **Physiological Monitoring:** Sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system arousal was continuously recorded via 3-lead ECG using an ADInstruments PowerLab system, measuring average Heart Rate (BPM) and Heart Rate Variability via RMSSD.

**3. Key Findings on the Role and Impact of AI**

**A. Experiment 1: AI Significantly Enhances Complex Brainstorming**

- **Outperformance Across All Metrics:** Multivariate analysis (MANOVA) revealed that the AI-augmented group significantly outperformed the web-search control group across all four evaluation categories (_p_ < .001):
    - **Originality (Rareness):** AI _M_ = 3.26 vs. Non-AI _M_ = 1.14 (_F_ = 47.48).
    - **Flexibility (Category Diversity):** AI _M_ = 7.15 vs. Non-AI _M_ = 4.36 (_F_ = 47.30).
    - **Fluency (Response Volume):** AI _M_ = 5.36 vs. Non-AI _M_ = 4.12 (_F_ = 18.42).
    - **Elaboration (Word Detail):** AI _M_ = 35.29 vs. Non-AI _M_ = 26.02 (_F_ = 13.98).
- **Single Underlying Creativity Factor:** Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) demonstrated that all four brainstorming metrics loaded onto a single underlying factor explaining **67.05% of total variance**.

**B. Experiment 2: AI Constrains Atomic Divergent Thinking (DAT)**

- **Reversal of Originality Gains:** In the single-word association task, Welch's ANOVA revealed a significant condition effect (_F_ = 247.81, _p_ < .001). Post-hoc Games-Howell tests showed that **Non-AI Humans achieved the highest Originality** (_M_ = 11.92), outperforming **AI-Augmented Humans** (_M_ = 10.96), while **AI-Alone performed the worst** (_M_ = 6.96).
- **Algorithmic Repetitiveness:** In the AI-alone condition, ChatGPT repeatedly generated common, statistically probable words across separate sessions (e.g., the word _"stone"_ appeared in 27 out of 50 independent sessions).
- **No Difference in Semantic Distance or Physiology:** Semantic distance (calculated objectively via WordNet's Jiang-Conrath method) showed no statistically significant differences across groups (_p_ = .266). Furthermore, AI use caused **no significant physiological stress shifts** in Heart Rate (_p_ = .789) or Heart Rate Variability (_p_ = .091) compared to human-alone task execution.

**C. External Factors & Correlations**

- **Attitudes & Semantic Distance:** Frequent usage of Generative AI (_r_ = .29, _p_ < .05) and positive attitudes toward AI (_r_ = .32, _p_ < .05) correlated positively with semantic distance scores during the DAT, suggesting that positive orientation encourages broader exploratory word selection.
- **Perceived vs. Actual Creativity:** In Experiment 1, perceived elaboration (_r_ = .28 to .35) and perceived flexibility (_r_ = .19 to .34) positively correlated with objective experimenter-coded creativity scores.

**4. Qualitative Nuances & Theoretical Implications**

- **Task-Dependent Paradigm Split:** Generative AI acts as a powerful creative co-pilot during complex, elaborative brainstorming by providing structural range and contextual depth. However, for simple divergent thinking tasks requiring highly unique single-word associations, AI hurts human performance because LLMs are statistically optimized to predict conventional, highly probable next words.
- **No Physiological Frustration Penalty:** Unlike earlier programming or gaming studies where AI tools increased user stress, using conversational AI for brief creative tasks did not alter heart rate or parasympathetic tone.
